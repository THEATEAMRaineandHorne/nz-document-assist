Apostille meaning

Apostille is a French word for a note added in the margin of a document. In law it means the certificate set out in the Hague Convention of 5 October 1961 Abolishing the Requirement of Legalisation for Foreign Public Documents, usually called the Apostille Convention.

Before the Convention, a document going abroad had to be legalised in a chain: by its own government, then by the destination country's embassy. The apostille replaces that chain with one certificate from one authority. New Zealand has been part of the Convention since 22 November 2001.

What does an apostille look like?

Every apostille follows the same model from the Convention. It is headed "Apostille (Convention de La Haye du 5 octobre 1961)" and has ten numbered items:

#Item#Item
1Country (New Zealand)6Date
2Signed by (the official or notary)7By (the issuing authority)
3Acting in the capacity of8Apostille number
4Bears the seal or stamp of9Seal or stamp of the authority
5Certified at (place)10Signature

A New Zealand paper apostille is a covering page attached to your document with an embossed seal. An e-apostille is the same certificate as a secure PDF. Many overseas offices only accept one or the other, so we check before lodging. See Paper vs E-Apostille.

What an apostille does not do

  • It does not check or confirm what the document says, only who signed or sealed it
  • It does not translate the document
  • It does not correct a wrong name or date
  • It does not oblige the receiving office to accept the document for its purpose; that office still sets its own rules
  • It is not accepted in countries outside the Convention, which need authentication instead

Who issues apostilles in New Zealand?

Only the Department of Internal Affairs. JPs, lawyers, Notaries Public and embassies cannot issue a New Zealand apostille. But not every document can be apostilled as it is: some need a new certificate, some a Notary Public first, and the wrong one is sent back. We make sure yours is right before it's lodged. Which New Zealand documents can be apostilled?

Most public and many private documents, including:

  • Birth, marriage, death and name change certificates (birth, marriage)
  • Criminal record and police certificates (criminal record)
  • Degrees, transcripts, NZQA and school records (education)
  • Court documents and divorce papers (divorce)
  • Powers of attorney and other private documents (power of attorney)
  • Passport copies (passport copy) and company documents

Each has its own rules about what form it must be in. Tell us what you have and we'll tell you what it needs. See NZ Apostille Requirements.

Countries that joined recently

The Convention keeps growing, so older advice can be wrong. China joined in November 2023, Canada in January 2024 and Vietnam on 11 September 2026. Documents for these countries now take an apostille, not authentication.

Frequently asked questions

What does apostille mean?

An apostille is a government certificate confirming that the signature, seal or stamp on a public document is genuine, so the document is accepted in Hague Apostille Convention countries without further legalisation.

How do you pronounce apostille?

Ap-uh-STEEL. It comes from French.

Who issues apostilles in New Zealand?

Only the Department of Internal Affairs. JPs, lawyers, notaries and embassies cannot issue a New Zealand apostille. We prepare and lodge it for you.

Is an apostille the same as notarisation?

No. A notary certifies or witnesses a document. An apostille is a government certificate that verifies the signature or seal on it, sometimes the notary's own.

Does an apostille expire?

The Convention sets no expiry date. But the receiving office may want a recently issued document, which we check for you.
